# Trust, Consent and User Agency

This reference owns user-facing understanding and control. It does not determine privacy policy/enforcement, security architecture, data governance, legal sufficiency or billing/business rules.

## Policy and presentation

| Question | Owner |
| --- | --- |
| What should users see, understand or control? | Interface design, using authoritative policy/product facts |
| What data is collected, shared, retained, and why? | Product/privacy/data owner |
| What enforces permission or security? | Security/platform/backend owner |
| What legal basis or disclosure is sufficient? | Current legal/policy authority |

Unresolved policy cannot be settled through polished copy. Design the transparent choice from known facts; return a missing policy decision to its owner. These principles do not establish that an interface is legally compliant or illegal. Retrieve exact applicable legal/accessibility requirements when material, including authentication, repeated entry/help, consent, cancellation or consequential commitment.

## Permission in context

Ask when users can understand the capability's relationship to their task. Explain purpose and scope truthfully when needed; convenient setup is not a reason for premature broad requests. Optional permission should not make the whole product appear unusable when only one feature is affected.

On denial, explain the affected capability and available next step without repeated pressure. When the platform owns the permission dialog, design surrounding context rather than imitating protected system UI. Current platform authority owns APIs, prompt behavior and settings routes.

## Meaningful choice and consequence

Name choices by understandable consequence. Make meaningful accept/decline, enable/disable, subscribe/cancel or share/not-share paths discoverable, with defaults grounded in current policy. Do not add friction, confusing toggles or visual imbalance primarily to defeat the user's valid alternative. Show the active state and a discoverable change/revoke route when supported.

Before consequential commitment, identify affected scope and material consequences. Separate preview/review from commitment where it lowers error cost; use confirmation proportionally and undo/recovery when the product can safely provide it. Keep cancel/escape available before commitment and explain completion and available recovery afterward. An engagement objective does not authorize obscured choice.

## Deceptive or obstructive behavior

Watch for hidden fees, recurring behavior, privacy consequences or scope; promotion disguised as independent/required content; intentionally difficult refusal/cancellation/deletion; unsupported scarcity or urgency; double negatives and ambiguous toggles; valid choices met with repeated nagging; permitted options made to appear unavailable; or optional purchases/data sharing added without authorizing user action.

A real state or policy change may create a new decision; ordinary persistence of a commercial preference does not. Resolve a conflict with business preference through a transparent choice model, returning policy or scope questions when needed.

## Blocked states

Distinguish missing prerequisites/input, lack of role/permission, temporary processing/offline/failure, and product/policy prohibition. Give the truthful reason and useful recovery near the action when it matters. A disabled control alone does not explain policy, and a permitted choice must not merely look disabled to discourage it.

## Decision-relevant privacy copy

Communicate the smallest truthful set needed now: the capability/data concerned, who or what uses it at the supported level of detail, purpose, effect of enabling/refusing, and how to change the choice when possible.

Do not invent retention periods, recipients, anonymity/security guarantees or legal bases. Secondary detail can sit behind a clear route; information that materially changes the immediate choice must remain available at that decision.
